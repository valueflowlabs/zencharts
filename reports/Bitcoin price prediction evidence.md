# Forecast Bitcoin's risk, not its price

Bitcoin's direction is barely predictable, and the little predictability that survives rigorous testing is short-horizon trend: past returns and price-to-moving-average ratios forecast returns days to weeks ahead. Even that evidence comes mostly from 2011–2018 samples and shows signs of decay. Almost everything else people use to call BTC tops and bottoms rests on three or four market cycles and does not survive honest out-of-sample tests. That covers Google searches and the Fear & Greed index, macro liquidity, halving cycles, stock-to-flow, on-chain thresholds such as MVRV, and deep-learning price models. **What can be forecast is risk.** BTC volatility clusters and persists, so realized-volatility models (HAR beats GARCH) give usable ranges, and Deribit's DVOL index shows the options market's own 30-day estimate alongside them. Leverage gauges are also informative: a high futures basis, high funding and record open interest predict the size and likelihood of crashes, not rallies. The only long-horizon level model with walk-forward support is the log-log power law, and it works as a wide 12–24-month trend anchor, not as a law. For zencharts, the practical answer is to build **probabilistic overlays** (a volatility cone, a power-law corridor) and **fragility panes** (funding, DVOL). Then score each one in-app against a naive random-walk baseline before trusting it, instead of drawing price targets.

## Trend is the one durable return signal, and it is fading

The strongest academic evidence for predicting BTC returns is time-series momentum. Liu and Tsyvinski's peer-reviewed study covers BTC from 2011 to mid-2018 ([NBER](https://nber.org/papers/w24877); [RFS via RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v34y2021i6p2689-2727..html)). It finds that a one-standard-deviation rise in today's return predicts **+0.33% the next day and +0.50% six days ahead**. A one-SD rise in this week's return predicts **+3.16% to +3.66% per week for the following one to three weeks**. The top quintile of weekly returns earned 11.22% a week in the following week, against 2.60% for the bottom quintile ([NBER PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24877/w24877.pdf)). The effect is smaller but still significant from 2013 onward, and it survives a "no-lookahead" quintile test. That test is a weak stand-in for a rolling out-of-sample forecast. Two other peer-reviewed papers confirm the result with explicit out-of-sample work. Detzel et al. show that **price-to-moving-average ratios over 5 to 100 days predict daily BTC returns both in- and out-of-sample** and yield economically meaningful Sharpe gains over buy-and-hold ([Financial Management via RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/finmgt/v50y2021i1p107-137.html)). Cheah et al. find that time-series momentum, economic-policy uncertainty and financial uncertainty beat the other popular predictors in-sample, out-of-sample and in asset-allocation tests ([European Journal of Finance via RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/eurjfi/v28y2022i1p66-85.html)). Hong reports that BTC momentum lasts about one to eight weeks and then partly reverses ([DOI](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/s10799-016-0264-6)).

Those effect sizes should be read as upper bounds. Every canonical sample ends between 2016 and 2018 and is dominated by the 2013 and 2017 bubbles. A seminar paper covering 3,100 coins finds that time-series momentum "seems to have disappeared after the introduction of bitcoin futures" in December 2017 ([Tinbergen seminar](https://utilities.tinbergen.nl/?p=66235)). Molnar's July 2026 preprint finds that popular cycle and price indicators worked for about a decade and then degraded, with short-horizon signals decaying toward zero and some flipping sign ([arXiv 2607.26188](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26188)). One unreviewed working paper using EMA signals on large coins from 2020 to October 2025 still calls momentum "economically meaningful" ([journals.vu.lt](https://www.journals.vu.lt/BATP/lt/article/download/44540/42590/138419)). **No peer-reviewed BTC-only momentum replication with a sample ending after 2022 turned up.** The efficiency literature tells the same story. BTC was clearly inefficient over 2010–2016 ([Urquhart, Economics Letters](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/400597/1/Bitcoin_20efficiency_20R_26R.docx)), and efficiency has varied over time since about 2014, with inefficiency clustering in bubble and crash episodes ([review, arXiv 2208.07254](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.07254)). That is the adaptive-markets picture: predictability shows up in episodes rather than as a constant edge. In practice, trend rules earn most of their value by **cutting drawdowns**, not by adding raw return.

Attention and sentiment look like momentum seen from another angle. Liu and Tsyvinski report that a one-SD jump in Google "Bitcoin" searches above their four-week average predicts **+1.84% next-week returns**, and Twitter post counts predict +2.50% ([NBER PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w24877/w24877.pdf)). Other studies weaken the causal reading. A copula Granger test finds the causality runs both ways and lives only in the tails ([Dastgir et al., FRL](https://research.tees.ac.uk/en/publications/the-causal-relationship-between-bitcoin-attention-and-bitcoin-ret/)). A CESifo paper finds that Google queries are cointegrated with BTC but "not helpful in predicting" it ([CESifo WP 7675](https://www.cesifo.org/DocDL/cesifo1_wp7675.pdf)). Searches spike with price, so the one-to-two-week signal largely overlaps past returns. No post-2018 study shows attention adding out-of-sample value after controlling for momentum. The Fear & Greed index has even weaker support: one year of weekly data in a Granger test ([Review of Behavioral Finance](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/RBF-08-2023-0224/full/html)), and in-sample contrarian backtests ([Springer chapter](https://ideas.repec.org/h/spr/eurchp/978-3-031-36286-6_16.html)). About half its inputs are derived from price ([Spark glossary](https://www.spark.money/glossary/fear-greed-index)). Practitioner tallies even show larger 90-day forward returns after "Extreme Greed" streaks than after "Extreme Fear" streaks ([MEXC News, unverified](https://www.mexc.com/news/1197268)). **"Buy extreme fear" is therefore not an evidence-backed rule.** Fear & Greed extremes behave more like a lagged momentum reading, which corrects the softer view in the project README. LLM-scored news is the newest version of the same idea, and it fits the same pattern. Fiszeder, Orzeszko and Pietrzyk find that ChatGPT-labelled headlines improve in-sample fit for BTC returns and volatility but give **no statistically significant out-of-sample gain** ([University of Warsaw seminar abstract](https://www.wne.uw.edu.pl/en/research/conferences-and-seminars/upcoming-events/lecture-researchers-nicolaus-copernicus-university-torun)).

Macro factors have peer-reviewed out-of-sample support only through uncertainty indices, in Cheah et al. Early studies with data through 2018 found BTC had no exposure to stock, bond, currency or commodity factors ([NBER](https://nber.org/papers/w24877)). Since 2020, BTC has moved with risk assets at the same time. Moving together is not predictability, and **no peer-reviewed out-of-sample evidence was found that DXY, real yields, M2 or "global liquidity" forecast BTC returns**. Molnar reports that M2 and yield-curve signals are unstable and lose a joint comparison against a simple time trend ([arXiv 2607.26188](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26188)). The popular "liquidity leads BTC by N weeks" charts are practitioner overlays with no published forecast test behind them. Day-of-week effects are also not actionable. Studies disagree on whether a Monday effect exists ([Caporale & Plastun](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/handle/2438/17208); [Kinateder & Papavassiliou](https://researchrepository.ucd.ie/entities/publication/ea776b97-3521-4fce-8a02-c5a9939b5563)), and trading simulations are "mostly not significantly different from random".

## Cycle models rest on three or four data points

Halving cycles, stock-to-flow and the power law all try to forecast the price level years ahead. All three are fitted to the same three or four bubbles, and their honest track records differ sharply.

The **halving cycle** is the most widely believed and the least testable. A peer-reviewed event study finds abnormal returns around halvings, but returns and volatility around them are **shrinking** as the market institutionalizes ([Veloso et al., JRFM 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jjrfmx/v18y2025i5p242-d1647669.html)). Molnar's preprint is the most careful timing analysis available ([arXiv 2607.26188](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26188)). Mature-cycle tops came **525, 546 and 534 days after halvings**, a cluster that appeared in none of 10,000 block-bootstrap paths. Bottoms came 364 to 406 days after tops, but that pattern appears in **31–43% of random paths**, so it is largely a generic property of drawdowns. The author calls the holdout evidence "suggestive not decisive". He pre-registered a **2026 bottom window of 5 October to 16 November**. That makes it a live out-of-sample test as of this writing (23 October 2026), and it can be scored after 16 November. Practitioner tables show peak multiples compressing each cycle, from about 95x in 2012 to about 30x in 2016 ([Lambda Finance](https://www.lambdafin.com/articles/bitcoin-returns-by-halving-cycle)). That is consistent with a fading effect.

**Stock-to-flow** has failed its own forecasts. PlanB's 2019 model regressed log market value on log stock-to-flow and implied about **$55,000 per BTC after the 2020 halving** ([CryptoSlate](https://cryptoslate.com/could-bitcoins-halving-push-its-price-to-55000-one-quant-thinks-so/)). Its "worst case" path of about $98k to $135k by late 2021 failed when BTC closed 2021 below $100k ([FXStreet](https://www.fxstreet.com/cryptocurrencies/news/bitcoin-stock-to-flow-model-invalidated-as-btc-closes-2021-below-100-000-202201010939); [Forklog](https://forklog.com/en/stock-to-flow-model-author-concedes-failure-of-the-worst-case-scenario-for-bitcoin/)). BTC then fell to about $17,600 in June 2022, breaking the model's lower band for the first time ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/opinion/three-reasons-why-planb-s-stock-to-flow-model-is-not-reliable)). The econometric critiques come from practitioners, and no peer-reviewed critique or validation was found. They argue that the model puts supply on both sides of the regression, that it regresses two trending series without detrending, and that it has no demand term ([Spark tracker](https://www.spark.money/tools/bitcoin-stock-to-flow-tracker); [Bitcoin Magazine](https://bitcoinmagazine.com/markets/why-bitcoin-stock-to-flow-is-not-useful)). A survey of the peer-reviewed literature concludes that S2F **failed formal out-of-sample testing** ([Baquero, arXiv 2606.00071](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00071)). Its deterministic halving step-ups have not been matched by price in the 2024–2026 epoch.

The **power law** (price ∝ days-since-genesis^β, with β ≈ 5.6–5.8) holds up better as a forecaster, though not as a structural claim. Baquero and Menezes test it formally ([arXiv 2605.21316](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21316)). The fitted exponent shifts by **nearly 3x depending on the chosen time origin**, residual diagnostics cannot tell it apart from a stack of sigmoids, and power-law tests reject it for return and balance distributions. Yet in walk-forward Diebold–Mariano tests against ten baselines, including random walk with drift, ARIMA and ETS, the **simple power law dominates at 12–24 month horizons (p < 0.05)**. The in-sample-best multi-sigmoid is among the worst long-horizon forecasters. Molnar also ranks the time-based power law as the most stable signal across epochs ([arXiv 2607.26188](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26188)). Critics call it overfit or a "horoscope" ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/bitcoin-power-law-debate-erupts-critics-call-it-a-magic-trick); [Zenodo preprint](https://zenodo.org/records/19975701)). Both sides can be partly right. A slowly bending log trend will fit any maturing adoption asset, and its corridor spans roughly an order of magnitude, which makes it hard to falsify. Mean reversion toward such a trend still beats naive extrapolation at long horizons. **Use it as a slow-moving reference band on 1D and 1W log charts, never for short-horizon returns or point targets.**

## Leverage and flows warn of crashes, but do not time rallies

Derivatives data says more about the **size and probability of drawdowns** than about direction. The best-supported finding comes from BIS Working Paper 1087 ([BIS](https://bis.org/publ/work1087.htm)). BTC futures carry has averaged **above 10% a year and reached 60%**, driven by trend-chasing demand for leverage and scarce arbitrage capital, and **high carry predicts future crashes**. Margin spikes and forced liquidation of arbitrage positions link leverage to the market's frequent severe drawdowns. The 10 October 2025 deleveraging fits the pattern, though the evidence is industry analysis. Amberdata reports that BTC open interest peaked at $54.7B and funding reached 29.9% APR beforehand. It also reports $2.3B of BTC liquidations in a day, 86% of them longs, and a cascade threshold near 5% of open interest ([Amberdata](https://blog.amberdata.io/leverage-liquidations-the-31b-deleveraging)). CoinDesk measured total crypto open interest falling 27.5%, with the steepest part inside 25 minutes ([CoinDesk Data](https://data.coindesk.com/blogs/how-ccix-performed-during-cryptos-largest-liquidation-event)). The bullish counterpart is that negative funding marks bottoms. K33's figure of **79% average subsequent returns after 30-day funding turns negative** rests on few independent episodes and an unverified method ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/315831/perp-signal-bitcoin-bottom-bullish-year-end-k33)). Option-implied measures are mainly useful for volatility. BTC's smile is much flatter and more symmetric than equity skew ([arXiv 2109.02776](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.02776)), and options activity is now split between Deribit, CME and IBIT, whose tail pricing diverges ([Kaiko](https://www.kaiko.com/news/kaiko-research-highlights-tail-risk-divergence-between-ibit-and-deribit-options)). No rigorous test of the put/call ratio was found, and "max pain" appears to be folklore.

Spot ETF flows mostly **follow price**. In DWF Labs' 694-day sample, daily flows correlate **0.54 with the prior five-day return and 0.03 with the next five days** ([Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/bitcoin-etf-flows-follow-price-trends-dwf-labs)). FalconX found a statistically significant flows-to-price Granger effect in 2024 data, but the impulse response peaks around **1.2% on days three to four**, and the authors flag the short sample and non-stationarity ([FalconX](https://www.falconx.io/newsroom/what-can-spot-etf-flows-tell-us-about-the-trajectory-of-bitcoin-prices-a-preliminary-statistical-investigation)). A peer-reviewed regression reporting R² of 95% between flows and price **levels** says little about forecastable returns ([Mazur & Polyzos](https://zuscholars.zu.ac.ae/works/7267)). Some ETF buying is also hedged CME basis trading rather than directional demand. The most direct flow evidence works at intraday horizons. USDT net inflows to exchanges positively predict BTC returns over one to six hours in 2017–2023 data, while BTC net inflows mostly do not. That preprint reports no out-of-sample metrics ([arXiv 2411.06327](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.06327)). Evidence that stablecoin issuance drives price is split, and issuance often reacts to price ([review, arXiv 2507.13883](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.13883)).

On-chain valuation metrics are economically meaningful but statistically fragile. A 2026 peer-reviewed backtest finds **MVRV Z-score trading rules beat NUPL, CVDD and buy-and-hold** ([Research in International Business and Finance](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0275531926002138)). It is a rule backtest over a handful of cycles, not a forecast test. Practitioner re-tests show NVT's edge "disappears almost entirely" when done properly ([Coinpaprika](https://coinpaprika.com/education/top-on-chain-metrics/)). Fixed thresholds have also broken in the ETF era. MVRV-Z peaked at **10.7, 10.1 and 7.2** in the 2013, 2017 and 2021 cycles but only **3.35 in December 2024**, so "Z > 6 means top" never fired, even though BTC later reached about $126k ([ArkFlow, practitioner](https://arkflow.substack.com/p/which-on-chain-metrics-still-work)). ETF custodians hold BTC outside standard exchange-reserve definitions, and custody shuffles look like whale accumulation. Coin Metrics revised **19 months** of its ETF wallet attribution in September 2026 ([Crypto Daily](https://cryptodaily.co.uk/2026/09/coin-metrics-bitcoin-etf-wallet-data-revision)). Activity metrics (exchange balances, NVT, active addresses, Metcalfe fits) have lost the most information. Cost-basis metrics such as MVRV and realized price keep more of it, but only with rolling-percentile thresholds rather than historical levels. No peer-reviewed tests were found for the Puell multiple, hash ribbons, dormancy or long-term-holder supply.

## Complex models rarely beat yesterday's price, but volatility is forecastable

Machine learning has not cracked BTC direction. Puoti, Pittorino and Roveri ran walk-forward tests on five major coins and found the series "closely resemble Brownian noise". **Naive models consistently outperformed** LSTM, N-BEATS and the other machine-learning and deep-learning models across horizons, though for BTC at one day the naive-drift and ARIMA errors are essentially tied ([arXiv 2502.09079](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09079)). Baquero's survey of peer-reviewed BTC prediction research concludes that **no study has shown robust superiority over the naive baseline across multiple regimes** at one-to-six-month horizons. Daily predictability "may not survive transaction costs" ([arXiv 2606.00071](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00071)). Where signals are real, they sit at the shortest horizons, which is where costs are largest. Jaquart, Dann and Weinhardt's one-to-sixty-minute classifiers beat random, and a long-short strategy earned up to **39% a month before costs, then lost money after them** ([KIT repository](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000150665)). Time-series foundation models do not change the picture on current evidence. A zero-shot benchmark of Chronos-2, TimesFM, Moirai and Lag-Llama on daily crypto returns found linear ridge regression as strong as the best of them. Adding sentiment features **worsened** point error ([POLITesi thesis](https://www.politesi.polimi.it/handle/10589/253774)). Reviews that conclude ML "outperforms traditional models" tally in-paper claims without auditing baselines ([arXiv 2511.21588](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21588)). They inherit publication bias, and a PRISMA review of the field flags a "lack of transparency and comparability" ([CEUR-WS](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-3791/paper26.pdf)).

The failures repeat in recognisable ways. The most common one is predicting price **levels**. An LSTM that outputs a horizontally shifted copy of yesterday's price looks accurate on RMSE because BTC is close to a random walk ([arXiv 2102.05448](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2102.05448)). A related study found lag 1 was the best input for both linear and neural models ([arXiv 2001.01127](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2001.01127)). Shuffled train/test splits leak the future into training ([arXiv 2401.13796](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.13796)). So do scalers fitted on the full sample ([temporalcv guide](https://temporalcv.readthedocs.io/en/latest/guide/common_pitfalls.html)). Trying many configurations and reporting the best one inflates results. The Deflated Sharpe Ratio corrects for the number of trials and for non-normal returns ([SSRN 2460551](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)). The Probability of Backtest Overfitting estimates how often the in-sample winner loses out of sample ([SSRN 2326253](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253)). Together these give a minimum standard. Evaluate chronologically with walk-forward windows. Hold out data from several regimes. Always include a random-walk or drift baseline, and test differences with Diebold–Mariano. Include transaction costs, and use proper scoring and calibration checks for probabilistic forecasts ([arXiv 2606.00071](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.00071)). Any BTC forecast reported as a price-level error with no naive baseline should be treated as uninformative.

Volatility is the exception, because it clusters and persists. Bergsli, Lind, Molnár and Polasik find that **HAR models on intraday realized variance beat GARCH on daily data**, most clearly at short horizons ([RIBAF 2022 via RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/riibaf/v59y2022ics0275531921001616.html)). A *European Financial Management* study finds that adding jumps, structural breaks and realized quarticity (HARQ-F-J) improves out-of-sample accuracy further ([Reading eprint](https://reading-clone.eprints-hosting.org/87932)). Studies that rank GARCH first, such as Chi and Hao, simply did not test HAR. Their GARCH also badly under-forecast the March 2020 crash ([arXiv 2010.07402](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.07402)). Implied volatility adds some information but does not settle the question. Dutta shows that jump-driven realized volatility carries **information beyond the Bitcoin implied-volatility index** ([Annals of Data Science](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40745-024-00517-4)). One study reports only a 0.48 correlation between a BTC implied-vol index and historical volatility ([arXiv 2109.15051](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.15051)). Alexander and Imeraj document a BTC variance risk premium across maturities ([Sussex](https://sro.sussex.ac.uk/id/eprint/91094/)), which means implied volatility typically sits above the volatility later realized. Machine learning sometimes edges HAR on one loss function but not significantly, and rankings flip between MSE and QLIKE ([Erasmus thesis](https://thesis.eur.nl/pub/63410/Final_version_thesis_Johan_Verschoor.pdf)). **Volatility forecasts support honest ranges and position sizing. They do not imply anything about direction.**

## What zencharts should build, and how to test each piece

The evidence points to one design rule. **Show distributions and fragility, then let the app grade itself.** zencharts already has the right base: daily history back to 2011 through the Bitstamp backfill, a pure-function indicator module for momentum, console-toggled overlays persisted to config, and a fake bottom pane built with `scaleMargins` on lightweight-charts 4.2.3. Every new feature below follows that pattern: a function on `Candle[]` or on a normalised `{time, value}[]` series, a server route mirroring `lib/sources.ts`, and a console toggle. All the data sources below answered without a key in curl checks on 23 October 2026, run from a Polish IP.

| Feature | Data (keyless, verified) | Hypothesis to test in-app | Baseline and pass criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Volatility cone** (`cone on`, `cone model ewma\|garch\|dvol`) | Existing candles; Deribit `public/get_volatility_index_data` (daily DVOL from 2021-03-24, 1,000 rows per call) | Fat-tailed quantile bands are calibrated: the 80% band is breached on about 20% of bars | Score EWMA (λ = 0.94), GARCH(1,1) and DVOL-scaled bands with QLIKE and breach rate. Pass if breach rates sit within a few points of nominal in 2017+ and post-2024 subperiods |
| **Power-law corridor** (`powerlaw on`, 1D/1W, log scale) | Existing daily closes since 2011 | Expanding-window fit beats random walk with drift at 12 months | Refit using data ≤ t only; compare 12-month log errors with a Diebold–Mariano test. Show how the slope drifted over time next to the corridor |
| **Trend filter backtest** (`backtest ma200`, `backtest tsmom`) | Existing daily closes | MA200 / 12-month TSMOM cuts max drawdown at similar or slightly lower CAGR | Against buy-and-hold over identical windows (2014+, 2017+, Jan 2024+), 10 bp per side. Pass on drawdown and Sharpe, not on CAGR alone |
| **Funding / OI fragility pane** | Binance `fapi` funding since 2019-09-10; OKX/Bybit via server proxy (no CORS); Binance OI only 30 days | Top-decile funding predicts larger 30-day max drawdowns, not lower average returns | Conditional drawdown distribution for top-decile vs other days. Pass if the drawdown gap holds in both pre- and post-2024 halves |
| **DVOL pane** | Deribit DVOL (CORS `*`) | DVOL forecasts 30-day realized vol, with a positive premium | Mincer–Zarnowitz style regression of realized on implied vol; report the average gap |
| **Fear & Greed strip** | `api.alternative.me/fng/?limit=0` (daily since 2018-02-01, CORS `*`) | Extremes add forward-return information **after controlling for 30-day momentum** | Regress 30/90-day forward returns on F&G extremes plus past return. Expect failure; keep as context only if it adds nothing |
| **MVRV pane** | CoinMetrics Community `CapMVRVCur` (from 2010; about 2-day lag; `CapRealUSD` is not free) | Rolling-percentile MVRV flags drawdown risk better than fixed thresholds | Rolling 4-year percentile vs fixed Z > 6; lag 2 days in all tests |
| **Halving window check** | mempool.space block height | Molnar's pre-registered bottom window of 5 Oct – 16 Nov 2026 holds | Read the realized low after 16 Nov 2026; print the result in the console |

Four implementation rules make these tests honest, following the evaluation standards above. First, a signal computed on close *t* is traded from bar *t*+1, and the bar still being live-polled is excluded. A unit test should confirm that changing `closes[t+1..]` never changes the signal at *t*. Second, every external series is lagged by its publication delay: about two days for CoinMetrics, one day for Fear & Greed. Third, parameters such as MA length or lookback are chosen walk-forward on past data only, and the results are shown next to the literature defaults (MA200, 12-month TSMOM). Fourth, every report prints its baseline (buy-and-hold for strategies, random walk for forecasts), its fee assumption, the number of variants tried, and per-subperiod results, so a single good window cannot pass for an edge. Cones should be centred on the current price with zero drift, or −σ²/2 for a median-preserving cone, because a fitted drift slips a direction forecast back in. Build the bands from empirical standardised-residual quantiles rather than Gaussian z-scores, because BTC tails are fat. The GARCH(1,1) multi-step variance bends toward its long-run level, σ²ₜ₊ₖ = σ̄² + (α+β)^(k−1)(σ²ₜ₊₁ − σ̄²). An EWMA cone simply widens with √h. A hand-written GARCH fit with Nelder–Mead takes about a hundred lines and runs in milliseconds on about 5,000 daily returns. The `garch` npm package claims HAR-RV and realized-GARCH variants, but it was not reviewed and it forecasts only one step ahead ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/garch)). HAR-RV needs intraday realized variance from hourly or 5-minute klines, and how much intraday history the app can fetch cheaply was not checked. That makes HAR a second step after EWMA and GARCH. TensorFlow.js and ONNX Runtime Web are not justified given the naive-baseline evidence.

Several engineering details are unverified and need a quick spike before committing to them. Cones and the power-law corridor need future-dated points. lightweight-charts has no future-axis API, so the community workaround appends future-dated whitespace or line points to a carrier series ([GitHub discussion #1462](https://github.com/tradingview/lightweight-charts/discussions/1462)). That this renders correctly in v4.2.3 is expected from the union time scale but was not confirmed. The v4.1 option controlling scroll when real bars replace whitespace was identified only from a search snippet. Upgrading to lightweight-charts v5 becomes worthwhile once two or more bottom panes exist (momentum plus DVOL, funding or an equity curve). Its native panes replace the hand-tuned margin constants ([migration guide](https://tradingview.github.io/lightweight-charts/docs/migrations/from-v4-to-v5); [panes](https://tradingview.github.io/lightweight-charts/docs/panes)). Check whether v5 keeps the attribution-logo option used to hide the TradingView logo before migrating. Two deployment constraints also apply. Vercel functions default to the **`iad1` US region** ([Vercel docs](https://vercel.com/docs/functions/configuring-functions/region)). Developers report Binance returning **HTTP 451** to US cloud IPs ([Vercel community](https://community.vercel.com/t/binance-api-calls-on-vercel/29895)), so pin server routes to `fra1` or call the CORS-enabled sources from the browser. CoinMetrics Community data is **CC BY-NC**, so attribution is required and commercial use is not permitted ([CoinMetrics API docs](https://docs.coinmetrics.io/api/v4/)). Daily sources should be cached for one to six hours with `s-maxage` headers, which keeps the app well inside CoinMetrics' documented limit of 10 requests per 6 seconds.

## Conclusion

The way to answer "how can one predict Bitcoin's price" has changed. Predictors of direction are weak, decaying and mostly fitted to bubbles that will not repeat on the same scale. Forecasts of the distribution are where real skill lives: how wide the next month's range is, how fragile current leverage is, and how far price sits from a slow long-run trend. The same lesson runs through every evidence class. Cycle thresholds broke when ETFs changed custody, momentum faded after futures arrived, and ML gains disappear once a naive baseline is included. Each time, a signal that worked in one market structure lost value when the structure changed. A forecasting feature is therefore only as good as its most recent out-of-sample period.

For zencharts, that argues for a small, self-auditing toolkit rather than a large indicator catalogue. A calibrated volatility cone, a power-law band refit honestly, and a funding or DVOL fragility pane each answer a question the evidence supports. A console backtester that always prints the naive baseline lets the app catch its own decay. The first concrete check is close: Molnar's pre-registered bottom window closes on 16 November 2026, and scoring it is a cheap first entry for that in-app record.
